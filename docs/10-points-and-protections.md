# Points and protections

Points are a second currency, not a discount. They beat cash when the **net** value survives taxes and surcharges; they lose when a "glamorous" business award carries $650 of fees. Consumer-protection rules are the part of cheap travel that pays you back after the trip has gone wrong.

---

## The only points math that matters

```
net cents per point = (realistic cash fare − unavoidable award taxes and surcharges)
                      ÷ points transferred × 100
```

Example: a $1,200 fare, 60,000 points and $650 in fees returns **0.92¢** — worse than cash if your floor is 1.5¢.

Rules:

- Use points only when net CPP exceeds your conservative valuation and beats the next-best use.
- Compare the same cabin, dates and flexibility. Do not compare an award to a fully refundable cash fare.
- Add opportunity cost: cash tickets earn miles and status; awards usually do not.
- Do not transfer until you have confirmed award space. Transfers are irreversible.
- Calculate transfer bonuses on the points you **transfer**, not the points that land.
- Portal redemptions are the baseline: Chase ~1.25–1.5¢, Amex ~1¢, Capital One ~1¢, Bilt ~1.25¢. Transfer only when the award beats the portal.

### Stopovers and companion passes

- **Aeroplan:** one stopover per direction, +5,000 points, up to 45 days, not within Canada or the US.
- **Flying Blue:** one complimentary stopover per award, phone booking only.
- **Alaska Atmos:** one enroute stopover per one-way on eligible international itineraries.
- **United:** Excursionist Perk ended for bookings on or after 21 August 2025.
- **Companion passes:** Southwest (135,000 qualifying points), Alaska Ascent and Summit, Delta Platinum and Reserve, American, BA Travel Together, JetBlue Plus. Terms and fare-class limits matter more than the headline.

Status matches are usually **challenges**: 60–120 days to hit a reduced target. Once per lifetime (Alaska) or once every three years (Delta). Current status only; card-earned status may be excluded.

## Protections worth knowing before you need them

| Right | What it gives you | Trigger and deadline |
|---|---|---|
| US 24-hour rule | Full refund within 24h, or a 24h hold at the quoted price | Booked direct, at least 7 days before departure; the airline chooses which |
| US DOT refund rule | Automatic refund on cancellation or a significant schedule change, even on non-refundable fares | 3h domestic / 6h international change; 7 business days for card refunds |
| EU261 | €250 / €400 / €600 by distance | Arrival 3+ hours late, unless extraordinary circumstances |
| UK261 | £220 / £350, then £260 or £520 over 3,500 km | Same 3-hour arrival trigger; escalate after 8 weeks |
| EU rail 2021/782 | 25% at 60–119 minutes; 50% at 120+ | Paid within one month; strikes and infrastructure failures do not exempt the operator |
| UK Delay Repay | 25–100% depending on operator and delay band | Claim within 28 days; season tickets pro-rated |
| US chargeback (Reg Z) | Dispute a charge for a service not delivered as agreed | Written notice within 60 days of the statement |

**Timing note:** the new EU air-passenger regulation (2026/2202) was published in October 2026, but its substantive provisions apply from **23 October 2027**. It adds a 9-month claim deadline, 30-day payment, automatic refunds, a self-reroute right after three hours, and no-show protection. UK261 is unaffected. Do not quote the 2027 rules as current rights.

### Denied boarding

The airline must first call for volunteers. If you are bumped against your will and checked in on time, you get compensation under Article 7 immediately, plus the choice of refund or re-routing, plus assistance. There is no reduction for denied boarding; the 50% reduction applies only where re-routing is offered.

### Chargebacks

Two frameworks in the US:

1. **Fair Credit Billing Act / Regulation Z** — billing-error dispute for services not accepted or not delivered as agreed. Written notice within 60 days; bank resolves within two billing cycles or 90 days.
2. **DOT airline refund rule** — automatic refund when the airline cancels or makes a significant change and you reject alternatives. Stronger than a chargeback, and separate.

Do not accept a voucher if you want cash; accepting one can waive the refund. Do not file a chargeback while an OTA refund is in progress.

## Third-party OTAs: the trade

Third-party agencies are the airline's customer, not yours. Schedule changes, refunds and rebooking flow airline → OTA → you, and during disruption the airline prioritises its own ticket holders.

Evidence:

- **Which?** across 28 cases: OTA headline fares were only marginally cheaper, but extras were always cheaper with the airline. A £2 saving became **£102 more** after luggage and seats on a Gatwick–Orlando trip.
- **Korea Consumer Agency:** across 800 fare checks at 10 OTAs, OTA tickets were more expensive than airline direct in **71.4%** of cases; cancellation fees were equal or cheaper only 10.3% of the time.
- **eDreams:** Italy's AGCM fined the company **€9M** in February 2026 over Prime subscription dark patterns.
- **Kiwi:** legitimate but high-risk. The guarantee often pays Kiwi Credit, not cash; service fees are non-refundable; an Austrian court invalidated 31 of its T&Cs in April 2026.
- **Gotogate/Etraveli:** EU commitments require refunds within ~14 days, but The Guardian documented a 16-month wait.

**Book direct when:** the itinerary is long-haul, connecting, basic economy, changeable, or tight; you need the 24-hour right; or the saving is small (~$30–50).

**An OTA can make sense when:** it is a simple single nonstop with a genuinely large saving, it mixes airlines that do not sell each other's flights, the OTA is established with clear ticket issuance, and you pay by credit card.

If you use one: verify who issues the ticket, read the change/refund rules, watch pre-ticked subscriptions, avoid relying on a self-transfer guarantee, get the airline PNR, and request cash to the original card in writing.

## Self-transfer insurance usually doesn't cover you

Most travel policies require one continuous itinerary or a single confirmation number, and explicitly exclude separately booked segments. Credit-card trip-delay cover typically starts at 6–12 hours with a $300–500 cap. Kiwi's guarantee often pays credit. Parametric products such as LayoverGuard pay automatically from flight data; they are built for self-transfers.

If you self-connect: build 2–3 hours domestic and 4–6 hours international, carry on only, know your backup flights, and either buy explicit separate-ticket cover or accept that the misconnection is yours.

## Hidden-city, in one paragraph

It is a breach of the contract of carriage, not a crime. Airlines can cancel the rest of the ticket, refuse checked bags, bill the fare difference, void miles and terminate status. Carry-on only, one-way, no loyalty number, never on a return, never on a trip that matters. The $9.4M Skiplagged verdict was for copyright in booking content, not a ruling that hidden-city is legal.
