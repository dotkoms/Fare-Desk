# What doesn't work

Every one of these is still repeated in 2026. Most cost money indirectly, by sending you to the wrong search or the wrong purchase.

---

## Air

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| Incognito mode or clearing cookies lowers fares | Airlines price from fare buckets, not your browsing history. Repeated tests — including 100 identical searches — find no difference. Closed user groups (mobile-only, member, logged-in fares) are segmented products, not cookie-based discrimination. |
| A VPN gets a cheaper price | One 2026 test across 10 countries and 3 routes found a maximum saving of $9; most foreign servers were **more** expensive. Currency arbitrage is a storefront effect, not a VPN effect. |
| Book on Tuesday | No magic booking day. The effect is 1–3%; Expedia's 2026 data puts Friday lowest. Tuesday is cheap to **fly** domestically, which is a different claim. |
| Book exactly 21 days out | Google says ~39 days domestic and ~89 international; Expedia says 15–30 and 8–15; Kayak 21–30 and 7–14. Route and season dominate any universal number. |
| Last-minute flights are cheaper | Close-in fares usually rise. The cheap international window is weeks out, not days. Last-minute is for distress and standby. |
| Hidden-city ticketing is illegal | It is a breach of the contract of carriage — a civil matter, not a crime. That is not a defence against cancelled segments, refused bags or a stripped account. |
| You can check a bag on a hidden-city ticket | Bags are tagged to the ticketed final destination, as are gate-checked carry-ons. The method only works carry-on. |
| Error fares are always honoured | No law compels it. In the US a genuine mistake fare can be cancelled with a refund and documented reliance expenses. Refund is the baseline; the trip is the bonus. |
| Booking direct is always cheaper | False on price — OTAs can undercut. True on outcomes: changes, refunds, irregular operations and error fares are easier direct. Compare, then book direct when the gap is small. |
| Budget airlines aren't on Google Flights | Most are. Southwest is the notable holdout because it blocks third-party sites; Ryanair and Wizz coverage varies by market. |
| One-ways are always cheaper than returns | US domestic one-ways are generally half a round trip. International one-ways are often **more** than a return or an open-jaw. |
| Booking at 3am or from an iPhone changes the fare | No replicable evidence for either. Fare buckets update continuously; device-based pricing is folklore. |

## Rail

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| Private browsing gets cheaper train fares | French regulators found no fare modulation based on repeat searches or IP. Prices move because quota sells out. |
| Split ticketing always saves money | Splits are found on roughly 60% of UK journeys — meaning about 40% have no saving. National Rail explicitly says they are not guaranteed to be cheaper. |
| Split tickets keep through-ticket protection | DIY splits are separate contracts. EU 2021/782 only protects a ticket a vendor bundled as one journey. |
| The JR Pass is always worth it | At ¥50,000 the 7-day pass loses on Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka (~¥30,500). It wins on long multi-region loops; regional passes are often better value. |
| A Eurail/Interrail pass is the cheapest way | Reservation fees of €10–35 per high-speed seat add €100+ to a trip. Fixed-date advance tickets usually beat it in western Europe. |
| A night train always saves a hotel night | A couchette often does. A peak-price single sleeper can cost more than a four-star room. |
| First class is always much more expensive | First Advance is a separate bucket and can undercut a Standard Anytime or Off-Peak fare when Standard Advance has sold out. |
| EU261 applies to trains | EU261 is aviation law. Rail compensation is Regulation 2021/782: 25% at 60–119 minutes, 50% at 120+. |
| Delay Repay is automatic | A few operators auto-pay smartcard claims; most require a manual claim within 28 days. |
| The station is always cheaper than Trainline | The fare is usually identical; the difference is the retailer fee (59p–£2.79, sometimes waived in the app). |
| Rail replacement buses mean cheap fares | No systematic discount. Timetables change, journeys take longer, advance release can be delayed. |

## Coach

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| The coach is always cheaper than the train | It loses against 4–8-week advance rail and low-cost rail (FlixTrain, Ouigo, AVLO, Italo), especially once two extra hours are valued. |
| Last-minute coach tickets are cheapest | The peer-reviewed evidence on FlixBus says the opposite: fares rise with occupancy, and the last-week effect only partly offsets it. |
| Megabus $1 fares are broadly available | They are promotional, often one seat per departure, and carry a ~$4.99 booking fee. The 2026 headline offers are mostly $9.99 sales. |
| The Interflix or Eurolines pass still exists | Interflix is discontinued with no 2026 replacement; the French Eurolines entity was liquidated in 2020. |
| Chinatown buses are still the $15 hack | Fung Wah was shut in 2013 and ceased in 2015. Lucky Star survives mainly as a brand within Flix/Greyhound. |
| Coach stations are in the city centre | Many are edge terminals — Paris Bercy/Pershing, Berlin ZOB, suburban Paris stops — adding 30–60 minutes and €2–5 by metro, or €20–40 by taxi at night. |
| A night bus always saves a hotel night | Only around 6–12 hours. Under 5–6 you arrive at 2–4am; over 14 you should fly or break the trip. |
| You can stack the student discount with a promo | Student fares are country-specific and usually one per booking. The UK gives 20% via UNiDAYS; Czechia gives 50% on domestic routes. Neither stacks. |
| Google Maps or Omio finds the cheapest bus | Google Maps is GTFS-only: no fares, no booking, no transit waypoints. Omio and Rome2Rio have coverage gaps and commercial incentives. Verify at the operator. |

## The pattern

Almost every myth is a claim that you can beat price segmentation without accepting a restriction. The real levers are always one of: flexibility, itinerary structure, timing against a baseline, or total-cost discipline. If a trick promises a discount with no trade-off, it is usually either stale or measuring the wrong thing.
